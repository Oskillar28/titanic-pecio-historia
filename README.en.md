## 2. History: construction and maiden voyage

### 2.1 A race for luxury, not speed
In the early 20th century Cunard dominated the Atlantic with very fast ships (*Lusitania*, *Mauretania*). Instead of competing on speed, White Star Line bet on **size and comfort**: three almost identical giants, the *Olympic* class. Titanic was the second and the most luxurious.

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e1/Titanic_under_construction.jpg" width="600" alt="Titanic under construction">
  <br><sub><i>Titanic under construction in Belfast. Public domain.</i></sub>
</p>

### 2.2 Luxury on board
First class offered a grand staircase under a glass dome, a gymnasium, a heated swimming pool, Turkish baths, an à la carte restaurant and lounges decorated in historical styles. Third class, though modest, was better than on many ships of the time (cabins instead of large shared dormitories).

### 2.3 The “unsinkable” myth
The shipping line never officially used “unsinkable” as a slogan; technical and popular press described the subdivision as “practically unsinkable”. The ship could stay afloat with **up to 4 of its forward compartments flooded**, but no more.

**Confidence: 0.85.** The exact origin of the “unsinkable” label is debated among historians.

### 2.4 Key people
- **Edward John Smith**: captain, on his last voyage before retiring. Died in the sinking.
- **Thomas Andrews**: Harland & Wolff naval architect. He calculated that the ship would sink. Died.
- **J. Bruce Ismay**: White Star Line chairman. Survived by boarding a lifeboat and was heavily criticised.
- **Jack Phillips and Harold Bride**: Marconi wireless operators. Phillips died; Bride survived.
- **William Murdoch**: first officer on watch at the moment of the collision. Died.

## 3. The night of the sinking, minute by minute

| 23:39–23:40 | Lookout Frederick Fleet spots an iceberg dead ahead. Murdoch orders a turn and engines astern. The **starboard** side scrapes the ice for a few seconds. |
| ~23:50 | Water entering the **first 5–6 compartments**. |
| ~00:00 | Thomas Andrews inspects the damage and estimates the ship has **1–2 hours** left. |

### 3.1 SS Californian
*Californian* was stopped by ice a few kilometres away. Her wireless operator had gone to bed, and her crew saw rockets but did not react. The 1912 inquiries harshly criticised her captain, Stanley Lord. **Confidence: 0.85** (the exact distance is still debated: estimates range from ~10 to ~30 km).

### 3.2 Victims
| Source | Survivors | Dead |
|---|---|---|
| Modern historians (Wikipedia consensus) | 712 | ~1,496 |
| US inquiry (1912) | 705–706 | ~1,517 |
| International agreement on the wreck (2003) | — | 1,523 |

🧠 **Reasoning:** the figures vary because the 1912 passenger lists contained duplicates, false names, people who disembarked at Cherbourg or Queenstown and poorly recorded crew. The honest figure is **“about 1,500 victims”**. **Confidence in the order of magnitude: 0.98; in the exact figure: 0.6.**

**Survival by class (approximate):** ~61–62% in first, ~41–42% in second, ~24–25% in third and ~24% among the crew. Far more women and children survived than men because of the “women and children first” rule. **Confidence: 0.85** (figures agree across several sources; they vary by ±1 point depending on the death count).

## 4. Why did it sink? Analysis of causes

A disaster like this almost never has a single cause. Broken down by factor:

| Factor | What happened | Estimated weight | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Speed in an ice field** | Sailing at ~22 knots at night despite ice warnings. Common practice in 1912, but it reduced the reaction margin. | High | 0.9 |
| **Poorly handled ice warnings** | The wireless operators were Marconi employees, busy sending passengers' private messages. A key warning never reached the captain. | High | 0.85 |
| **Conditions that night** | Flat calm sea (no waves breaking at the base of the iceberg) and no moon: the ice was almost invisible. The lookouts had no binoculars. | Medium | 0.8 |
| **The manoeuvre** | By turning, the ship struck a glancing blow instead of a head-on collision. A head-on hit would probably have flooded fewer compartments (counterfactual hypothesis). | Debated | 0.5 |
| **Hull damage** | There was no “great gash”: the 2025 model (University College London) points to a **row of small openings**, each about the size of an A4 sheet, spread over 6 compartments. | Key | 0.8 |
| **Wrought-iron rivets** | Bow and stern used **wrought-iron** rivets with up to **~9% slag** (normal is 2–3%). They could pop and open the seams “like a zip”. | Medium-high | 0.7 |
| **Brittle steel in cold water** | The steel had a high **ductile-to-brittle transition temperature**: in ice-cold water it behaved more like glass than like flexible metal. | Medium | 0.65 |
| **Bulkheads that didn't reach the top** | The watertight bulkheads did not rise to the top deck: water spilled over from one compartment into the next, like a tilted ice-cube tray. | High | 0.9 |
| **Too few lifeboats** | Only 1,178 seats for 2,208 people. It was **legal** in 1912 (rules were based on tonnage, not on the number of people). | Decisive for the death toll | 0.95 |

🧠 **Reasoned conclusion:** the iceberg was the trigger; the combination of speed, ignored warnings, materials at their limit and a bulkhead design with a weak point turned the collision into a sinking. And the lack of lifeboats turned the sinking into a massacre. **Overall confidence: 0.85.**

## 5. Consequences: what changed at sea

- **SOLAS Convention (1914)**: *Safety Of Life At Sea*, the first international maritime safety treaty. It requires **lifeboats for everyone on board**. Still in force in updated versions.
- **International Ice Patrol** (1914): iceberg monitoring in the North Atlantic, today run by the US Coast Guard with aircraft.
- **24-hour wireless watch** mandatory on passenger ships.
- Evacuation drills and improved bulkhead design.

## 6. Discovery of the wreck (1985)

For 73 years nobody knew exactly where it was. Several sonar expeditions failed because the position radioed in 1912 was about 20 km off.

- **1 September 1985:** a French-American expedition led by **Jean-Louis Michel** (IFREMER, France) and **Robert Ballard** (Woods Hole, USA) finds it.
- **The military “cover”:** the US Navy funded Ballard to locate and photograph the remains of two **sunken nuclear submarines** (USS *Thresher* and USS *Scorpion*). Once that secret mission was complete, he had time left over to search for Titanic.
- **The key to success:** studying the submarines, Ballard realised that light objects form a very long **debris trail**. Instead of searching for the ship, he searched for the **trail**. With the **Argo** camera sled, towed from the research vessel *Knorr*, the first thing he saw was a **boiler**.

**Wreck coordinates:** 41°43′32″ N, 49°56′49″ W · **Depth:** ~3,800 m · **Distance from Newfoundland (Canada):** ~600 km.

**Confidence: 0.95.**

| Year | Who / vehicle | What was achieved | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1985 | Ballard and Michel / *Argo*, *Knorr* | Discovery | ✅ Success |
| 1986 | Ballard / submersible *Alvin* + robot *Jason Jr.* | First people at the wreck; interior photos; plaque asking that it be left alone | ✅ Success |
| 1987 | IFREMER + investors / submersible *Nautile* | 32 dives, **more than 1,800 objects** recovered | ⚠️ Controversial (“grave robbing” according to critics) |
| 1991 | Russia-Canada-USA / *MIR* submersibles | IMAX filming (*Titanica*) | ✅ |
| 1993–2000 | RMS Titanic Inc. (RMST) | Thousands of objects: engine part, whistle, telegraphs, perfume vials | ⚠️ Controversial |
| 1995 | James Cameron / *MIR* | 12 dives for the film *Titanic* (1997). During the expedition **the bulkhead of the captain's quarters collapsed** | ⚠️ Damage to the wreck |
| **1996** | RMST | Attempt to lift a ~20-tonne hull section: **the cables snapped** and it fell back to the seabed | ❌ Failure |
| **1998** | RMST | **Second attempt succeeds**. “The Big Piece” (~17 t, now divided into 15 t + 2 t) | ✅ Technical success |
| 2001 | Cameron, tourists | Documentary *Ghosts of the Abyss*; even a wedding in a submersible | ⚠️ |
| 2003–2004 | NOAA (with Ballard in 2004) | Scientific assessment of deterioration, stereo mapping | ✅ Science |
| 2005 | Documentary expeditions | Discovery of **two large pieces of the hull bottom**, confirming how the ship broke apart | ✅ |
| 2010 | RMST + Woods Hole / 2 AUVs | **First complete map** of the debris field; identification of *Halomonas titanicae* | ✅ Science |
| 2019 | *Limiting Factor* (Triton) | 4K footage; confirmed the collapse of areas such as the captain's quarters | ✅ |
| 2021–2023 | OceanGate / *Titan* | High-cost tourism (~$250,000 per seat) | ❌ Ends in tragedy |
| **2022** | OceanGate (scientists on board) | Video used for the first systematic study of the wreck's **megafauna** (published in 2025) | ✅ Science |
| **2022–23** | Magellan + Atlantic Productions | **Complete 3D scan** (made in 2022, unveiled in 2023): >700,000 images, a “digital twin” | ✅ Success |
| **2023** | *Titan* | **Implosion**: 5 dead | ❌ Tragedy |
| 2024 | RMST / ROV + AUV | >2 million photos; **the bow railing has fallen**; the **Diana of Versailles** statue rediscovered | ✅ / 📉 decay |
| 2025 | RMST | No expedition; lawsuit over recoveries withdrawn | — |
| **2026** | Norfolk court (USA) | Auction of ~100 objects blocked (see section 14) | ⚖️ |

### 7.1 The most important successes
1. **Finding it** (1985), solving where it lay.
2. **Proving it broke apart at the surface** (some witnesses said so in 1912 and were not fully believed).
3. **Studying the recovered metal**: allowed rivets and steel to be analysed in the lab (NIST, 1998).
4. **Documenting it entirely without touching it** (2023 3D scan and 2024 photos).
5. **Deep-sea science**: discovering a new bacterium and studying how animals colonise an artificial object.

### 7.2 Failures and mistakes
1. **The 1996 lift**: lost material and a damaged hull.
2. **Damage from visits**: submersibles have bumped into the structure and ballast weights have struck decks; NOAA documented impact marks.
3. **The Marconi radio project (2020)**: a judge allowed RMST to cut through the roof to remove the telegraph. The US opposed it under the 2019 treaty; with the pandemic and the litigation the mission **never happened**.
4. **Titan (2023)**: the greatest failure of all, with five lives lost.

**Table confidence: 0.85** (some per-expedition object counts vary by source).

## 8. The Titan submersible tragedy (2023)

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Titan_submersible_on_the_ocean_floor.jpg/960px-Titan_submersible_on_the_ocean_floor.jpg" width="420" alt="Titan debris on the seabed">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/47/Wreckage_of_Titan_submersible_aft_section.png" width="420" alt="Remains of Titan's aft section">
  <br><sub><i>Debris of the Titan submersible on the seabed at about 3,775 m: the tail cone and the remains of the aft section, filmed by a robot on 22 June 2023. Public domain (US Coast Guard).</i></sub>
</p>

### 8.1 What happened
- **18 June 2023**: OceanGate's *Titan* begins its descent at 9:14 from the vessel *Polar Prince*.
- At **3,346 m depth**, about 450 m above the seabed, it sends its last automatic message and **implodes**.
- Killed: **Stockton Rush** (OceanGate CEO), **Paul-Henri Nargeolet** (veteran Titanic explorer), **Hamish Harding**, **Shahzada Dawood** and his son **Suleman**.
- On **22 June**, a robot finds the tail cone and other debris **~500 m from Titanic's bow**.

### 8.2 Why (US Coast Guard report, August 2025)
The report concludes the tragedy **was preventable**. Main causes:
- A **carbon-fibre hull** of ~2.4 m glued to titanium end sections, a design never validated for repeated dives.
- **No lifecycle analysis** of the hull (fatigue accumulated over dozens of dives).
- “Real-time” monitoring whose data **was not really analysed**, and no preventive maintenance.
- It kept operating after incidents that had damaged the hull.
- A toxic work culture that silenced those who raised concerns; key staff were fired.
- It operated **outside US regulations** and without third-party certification.
- Had Rush survived, a criminal investigation would have been recommended.

🧠 **Technical reasoning:** carbon fibre is very strong in **tension** (being stretched), but a submerged hull works in **compression** (being crushed). Carbon also gives no warning: it can accumulate invisible micro-damage (delamination) and fail all at once. Titanium or steel, by contrast, deform before failing, and their behaviour has been studied for decades. **Confidence: 0.9.**

## 9. The full 3D scan: what the “digital twin” revealed

In summer 2022 (unveiled in 2023) the deep-sea mapping company **Magellan** and the production company **Atlantic Productions** spent more than 200 hours with two robots photographing every centimetre: **more than 700,000 images** and millions of laser measurements. The result is a 1:1 replica that can be “walked through” on a computer. It was presented in the documentary *Titanic: The Digital Resurrection* (2025).

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/79/Titanic_wreck_model.jpg" width="650" alt="Model of the wreck">
  <br><sub><i>Model of the wreck's bow, driven into the seabed, at an exhibition. (The official 3D scan images are copyrighted by Atlantic Productions/Magellan and are not reproduced here.)</i></sub>
</p>

**Main findings:**
1. **Concave boilers** in the boiler room visible at the bow's break point: suggests they **were still working** as they sank. This supports accounts that the engineers kept the lights on until the end (none survived).
2. **Damage simulation** (Prof. Jeom Kee-Paik, University College London): the iceberg opened **small holes** the size of an A4 sheet along a narrow strip affecting **6 compartments**. Small, but enough.
3. The exact impact area **cannot be seen**: it is buried under sediment, because the bow drove into the mud.
4. A **broken porthole** consistent with the iceberg strike (survivors reported ice coming in through a window).

**Confidence: 0.8.** The findings were presented by the researchers in a documentary; part of the analysis has not yet been published in peer-reviewed form.

## 10. Current state of the Titanic (2026)

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9c/Titanic_wreck_bow.jpg" width="700" alt="Titanic's bow on the seabed">

### 10.1 How it is laid out
| Part | Condition |
|---|---|
| **Bow** (~140 m) | Fairly recognisable. It drove into the seabed at high speed and buckled. Decks, portholes and interiors survive. |
| **Stern** (~105 m) | Destroyed. As it sank, trapped air burst the decks, then it hit the bottom almost vertically; it looks like “crushed scrap”. |
| **Separation** | About **600 m** between bow and stern. |
| **Debris field** | Two areas 600–800 m long, about **5 km²**: boilers, crockery, bottles, shoes, coal, pieces of hull. |

### 10.2 What has been lost in recent years
| Feature | What has happened |
|---|---|
| **Crow's nest** | Gone; it no longer exists. |
| **Captain Smith's quarters** | Outer bulkhead collapsed; his famous bathtub became covered by debris (thought lost in 2019; in 2021 it was seen to still be there, buried). |
| **Gymnasium** | Walls pushed inwards. |
| **Bow railing** (port side) | **Fallen to the seabed** (documented in 2024). This is where the famous film scene takes place. |
| **Midship decks** | Progressively collapsing. |

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/30/Captain_Smith%27s_bathroom.jpg" width="460" alt="Captain Smith's bathtub">
  <br><sub><i>Captain Smith's bathtub among the remains of his cabin. Public domain (Wikimedia Commons).</i></sub>

### 10.3 When will it disappear? (critical analysis)
| Who | Prediction |
|---|---|
| Henrietta Mann (co-discoverer of *Halomonas titanicae*), 2010 | Total collapse possible around **2030** |
| NOAA | The structure could collapse within **~50 years** |
| Roy Cullimore (microbiologist) | Rusticles would consume **half of the hull in ~200 years** |

🧠 **Reasoning:** the predictions disagree because they measure different things (“collapse” of decks ≠ disappearance of the iron). The 2024 photos show a still-recognisable bow, so **total collapse by 2030 seems unlikely**. Most realistic: the upper decks and superstructure will keep collapsing over the coming decades, but the thick hull of the bow, half buried in mud, will last much longer. **Confidence: 0.6** (nobody has a reliable model; it is a very complex biological, chemical and mechanical process).

## 11. The environment: what it is like at 3,800 metres

| Parameter | Approximate value | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Depth | ~3,800 m | More than 4 times the height of the Burj Khalifa |
| Pressure | **~380–390 bar** (~5,500 psi) | Every 10 m of water adds ~1 bar. Like having **a car resting on each fingernail** |
| Temperature | **~1–4 °C** | Bottom water originating in polar regions |
| Sunlight | **None** | Light does not penetrate beyond about 1,000 m. Total, permanent darkness |
| Seabed | Plain of mud and fine sand | Sediment accumulated slowly over millions of years |
| Currents | Weak but persistent | The Atlantic's deep western boundary current; occasional “benthic storms” that stir up the mud |
| Descent time | ~2 hours | The same to come back up |

🧠 **Calculation:** 3,800 m ÷ 10 m/bar ≈ 380 bar, + 1 bar of atmosphere ≈ 381 bar. 1 bar ≈ 1 kg per cm², so each square centimetre bears about **390 kg**. **Confidence: 0.95.**

## 12. Animals, microbes and “vegetation” on the wreck

### 12.1 Are there plants or algae? No
🧠 **Reasoning:** plants and algae need **light** for photosynthesis. At 3,800 m not a single photon of sunlight arrives. So there is **no vegetation**. What looks like “vegetation” (corals, sponges, sea pens, anemones) is actually **animals** fixed to the substrate. **Confidence: 0.99.**

So what do they live on? **Marine snow**: remains of plankton, faeces and dead organisms that slowly fall from the surface. Titanic is also an **artificial reef**: a hard surface in the middle of a mud plain, ideal for animals to attach to.

> 🔗 Related: in my study *Ocean trenches: life without light* (in Spanish) I explain chemosynthesis and life in total darkness.

### 12.2 The “iron eaters”: rusticles and *Halomonas titanicae*

<p align="center">
  <img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2f/Detached_rusticles_hires.jpg/960px-Detached_rusticles_hires.jpg" width="550" alt="Detached rusticles">
  <br><sub><i>“Rusticles” detached from the hull. NOAA / public domain.</i></sub>
</p>

- **Rusticles** (*rust* + *icicles*): icicle-shaped formations of rust hanging from the hull. They look like rock but are **living colonies** of bacteria, archaea and fungi that obtain energy by oxidising iron. They are so fragile they crumble when touched.
- ***Halomonas titanicae***: a bacterial species **new to science**, isolated from Titanic rusticles and described in 2010 by a team from Dalhousie University (Canada) and the University of Seville (Spain). It is **halophilic** (salt-loving) and is part of the ecosystem that is “eating” the ship.
- Microbes are estimated to remove around **~180 kg of iron per day** (a widely quoted figure that is hard to verify: **confidence 0.4**).

### 12.3 Animals observed (Cleland et al., 2025)
The first systematic megafauna study of the Titanic analysed 920 images of the wreck and 169 of a nearby seamount ridge (~40 km away), recorded in 2022. **99% of the images showed visible fauna.**

| Animal | What it is | Where it is seen |
|---|---|---|
| **White squat lobsters** | Crustaceans resembling small lobsters, more closely related to hermit crabs | Very abundant on the wreck and debris field |
| **Rattails** (grenadiers) | Large-eyed, long-tailed fish typical of the deep seabed | Over mud and sand |
| **Bamboo corals** (*Lepidisis* sp.) | Branching deep-sea corals | On the bow railings, the anchor crane and around portholes. **Some colonies have doubled in size in 24 years** |
| **White sea anemones** | Polyps without a skeleton | On the hull (2010 and 2022 video) |
| **Brittle stars and basket stars** | Relatives of starfish with thin arms | Decks and seabed |
| **Deep-sea starfish** | Filter feeders that extend their arms into the current | Bow |
| **Sea pens** | Feather-shaped colonies of polyps | Nearby seabed |
| **Sponges and barnacles** | Fixed filter feeders | Hull (1991–2001 records) |
| **Sea cucumbers** | Echinoderms that eat sediment | Mud plain |
| **Bacteria, archaea and fungi** | Rusticle builders | The whole hull |

**Reasoned observation:** on the nearby natural ridge, **sea urchins** (*Gracilechinus*) are abundant but **do not appear on the wreck**, while squat lobsters are more common on the wreck. The authors' conclusion: a shipwreck is not a copy of a natural habitat; it creates **its own community**. **Confidence: 0.8.**

## 13. What is on the Titanic: objects inside and around it

### 13.1 What is still on the seabed
- Giant **boilers** (the first thing Ballard saw).
- **Shoes and boots in pairs**: they mark where bodies fell. Flesh and bones were consumed (bones dissolve because at that depth the water is **undersaturated in calcium carbonate**), but leather **tanned with tannins** resists bacteria.
- Stacked **crockery** (plates sank still in their wooden racks, which have disappeared).
- Bottles of wine and champagne, some still sealed.
- **The Marconi wireless room**, inside the superstructure, with remains of the telegraph.
- **The Diana of Versailles statue** (bronze, ~60 cm), from the first-class lounge, photographed in 1986 and found again in 2024.
- Large pieces of the hull bottom, the anchor crane, capstans, anchors.
- Possible human remains in enclosed areas of the interior (engine rooms, third-class cabins). Most bodies are estimated to have disappeared by the 1940s.

### 13.2 What has already been raised
More than **5,500 objects** recovered (almost all by RMST since 1987), conserved and shown in touring exhibitions:

## 14. Touch the wreck? Raise objects? The great debate

### 14.1 2026 news: the auction blocked
In August 2026, RMST sought permission to **auction ~100 objects** (jewellery, cutlery, a bronze cherub from the grand staircase) recovered in 1987, arguing they were outside the court's jurisdiction. NOAA and the French government opposed it. On **25 August 2026**, Judge **Rebecca Beach Smith** (Norfolk, Virginia) **blocked the auction**: any sale requires court approval and the collection must stay together.

### 14.2 Should the whole ship be raised?
**No — and it cannot be.** Absurd ideas have been proposed (filling it with ping-pong balls, injecting 180,000 t of petroleum jelly, freezing it with liquid nitrogen so it floats like an iceberg). 🧠 The hull is so corroded it would **fall apart** if lifted, and the sudden change in pressure, temperature and oxygen would destroy what little remains. **Confidence: 0.99.**

### 14.3 Raise loose objects from the debris field?

| ✅ Arguments for | ❌ Arguments against |
|---|---|
| The wreck **is going to disappear**: if they are not rescued, they are lost forever. | It is a **grave** for ~1,500 people. Survivors such as Eva Hart called it “grave robbing”. |
| Millions of people have seen real objects in museums: **educational value**. | The 1987 recoveries were made for commercial purposes. |
| The objects provide **science** (metallurgy of rivets and steel, everyday life in 1912). | Removing an object **strips it of context**: archaeological information about where and how it lay is lost. |
| Debris-field objects are **loose**: collecting them does not damage the structure. | Risk of **trading and auctions** (as in 2026). |
| They keep specific victims' stories alive (a watch, letters). | **UNESCO** recommends **in situ preservation** as the first option. |
| | Every expedition carries human risk (Titan). |

### 14.4 Touch or cut the hull (for example, to remove the Marconi radio)?

| ✅ For | ❌ Against |
|---|---|
| The radio is **a unique historical object**: it transmitted the distress calls that saved 712 people. | It means **cutting** the roof of a structure that is already collapsing. |
| The superstructure will collapse anyway and the radio will be buried. | **Human remains** might still be inside. |
| | The US–UK agreement requires authorisation to enter the hull. |
| | A dangerous precedent: if it is cut once, why not again? |

### 14.5 My reasoned conclusion
1. **The whole ship:** leave it where it is. **Confidence: 0.99.**
2. **Hull and superstructure:** do not cut them. Document them with 3D scans before they collapse (already done in 2023). **Confidence: 0.85.**
3. **Loose objects in the debris field:** this is the genuinely debatable issue. A balanced solution would allow only **scientific or museum** recoveries, with no sales, the collection always kept together and public, and **never** in areas where human remains may lie. **Confidence: 0.6** (this is an ethical question; there is no “objective” answer).

## 15. Legal framework: who protects the Titanic

| Year | Law / decision | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| 1986 | *RMS Titanic Maritime Memorial Act* (USA) | Calls for the wreck to be designated an international memorial |
| 1994 | Federal court in Norfolk | Names RMST **salvor-in-possession**: only it may recover objects |
| 2003 | International agreement on the wreck (USA, UK, Canada, France) | Signed by the USA and UK; in force after ratification in late 2019 |
| 2012 | 2001 UNESCO Convention | On the 100th anniversary of the sinking, the wreck becomes protected as **underwater cultural heritage** |
| 2020 | Marconi radio case | Permission to cut “minimally”; the US opposes; never carried out |
| 2026 | Judge Rebecca Beach Smith | Blocks the auction of ~100 objects |

**Important nuance:** the wreck lies in **international waters**, so no country owns it. Protection depends on treaties between countries and on courts' control over companies within their jurisdiction. **Confidence: 0.85.**

## 16. Technical glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **RMS** | *Royal Mail Ship*. A ship authorised to carry British royal mail; a mark of prestige. |
| **Gross tonnage (GRT)** | A measure of a ship's **internal volume**, not its weight. 1 register ton = 2.83 m³. |
| **Length / Beam** | A ship's length and width. |
| **Keel** | The ship's “backbone” along the bottom; laying it is the official start of construction. |
| **Launch** | When the hull first enters the water (still unfinished). |
| **Bow / Stern** | Front / rear of the ship. |
| **Port / Starboard** | Left / right side when facing the bow. Titanic struck on starboard. |
| **Watertight bulkhead** | An internal wall dividing the hull into compartments so water cannot pass from one to the next. |
| **Superstructure** | Everything built above the main deck (bridge, upper cabins, funnels). |
| **Knot** | Nautical speed unit: 1 nautical mile per hour = 1.852 km/h. |
| **Triple-expansion engine** | A steam engine that reuses steam in three cylinders of decreasing pressure to gain efficiency. |
| **Parsons turbine** | A steam turbine that used exhaust steam from the engines to drive the centre propeller. |
| **Rivet** | A metal pin that is heated, inserted through two holes and hammered flat to join plates. Titanic had ~3 million. |
| **Wrought iron** | Nearly pure iron with threads of **slag** (glassy residue) inside. Too much slag makes it brittle. |
| **Slag** | Silicate impurities left inside the metal during manufacture. |
| **Ductile-to-brittle transition temperature** | The temperature below which a steel stops bending and starts **breaking like glass**. |
| **Charpy test** | A lab test in which a pendulum strikes a specimen to measure how much energy it absorbs before breaking. |
| **CQD / SOS** | Morse-code distress signals. CQD was Marconi's older one; SOS the new international one (easy to key: ··· −−− ···). |
| **Marconi telegraph** | The era's wireless that sent Morse code by radio waves. |
| **SOLAS** | *Safety Of Life At Sea*: the international maritime safety convention born after the Titanic. |
| **Side-scan sonar** | Towed sonar that “draws” the seabed with sound echoes on both sides. |
| **Argo** | A camera sled towed near the seabed, used in 1985. |
| **Submersible** | An underwater vehicle that depends on a support ship (unlike an autonomous submarine). |
| **ROV** | *Remotely Operated Vehicle*: an underwater robot controlled by cable from a ship. |
| **AUV** | *Autonomous Underwater Vehicle*: an underwater robot that navigates on its own along a programmed route. |
| **Magnetometer** | A sensor that detects metal by measuring disturbances in the magnetic field. |
| **Photogrammetry** | A technique that combines thousands of photos of an object to rebuild it in 3D. |
| **Digital twin** | An exact virtual replica of a real object, here the entire wreck at 1:1 scale. |
| **Hydrostatic pressure** | Pressure from the weight of water: it rises ~1 bar every 10 m. |
| **Implosion** | Inward collapse of a structure because external pressure exceeds its strength. Happens in milliseconds. |
| **Carbon fibre** | A composite of carbon fibres bonded with resin: very light and strong in tension, but hard to assess under repeated compression. |
| **Material fatigue** | Damage accumulated by repeated loads, even if each load is below the breaking load. |
| **Delamination** | Separation of the layers of a composite material; it can be invisible from outside. |
| **Salvor-in-possession** | A US legal status: a company with the exclusive right to recover objects from a wreck. |
| **In situ preservation** | Protecting heritage **where it lies**, without moving it. |
| **Rusticle** | A microbe-built rust formation shaped like an icicle. |
| **Halophilic** | An organism that needs salt to live. |
| **Gram-negative** | A type of bacterium with a thin cell wall and an outer membrane; it stains pink in a Gram stain. |
| **Biofilm** | A community of microbes stuck to a surface inside a “slime” they produce themselves. |
| **Archaea** | Microorganisms resembling bacteria but belonging to a separate evolutionary group. |
| **Benthic** | Living on or attached to the seabed. |
| **Megafauna** | In marine biology: animals large enough to be seen in photos or video (over ~1 cm). |
| **Abyssal plain** | A flat, deep ocean floor, between ~3,000 and 6,000 m. |
| **Seamount / ridge** | A rise in the seabed; a natural rocky area where corals attach. |
| **Marine snow** | A continuous rain of organic debris falling from the surface that feeds the deep seabed. |
| **Artificial reef** | A submerged human-made structure colonised by living things. |
| **Benthic storm** | An episode of strong currents on the seabed that stirs up sediment. |
| **Undersaturated in calcium carbonate** | Cold, high-pressure water that **dissolves** shells and bones instead of preserving them. |
| **Tannins** | Plant substances used to tan leather; they make it resistant to bacteria. |

## 17. Fact-checking and originality

### 17.1 How the facts were checked
- **Cross-checked against two or more sources:** depth and position of the wreck, victims and survivors, survival by class, Titan dates, findings of the 3D scan, the railing collapse in 2024, the auction blocked in 2026 and the authors of *Halomonas titanicae*.
- **From a single reliable source** (official report or scientific paper): the causes of the Titan loss (US Coast Guard report), the rivets (NIST) and the list of fauna (Cleland et al., 2025).
- **General knowledge, without a specific linked source:** the minute-by-minute timeline of the sinking and some details of the ship (binoculars, water temperature). These are widely accepted but were not checked one by one.
- Where sources **disagree** (victims, collapse predictions), all figures are shown with an explanation of why they differ.
- Facts that are widely repeated online but lack a clear study behind them (e.g. “microbes eat 180 kg a day”) are marked **low confidence**.

### 17.2 Is this text copied from elsewhere?
- **The facts** (dates, figures, names) necessarily appear in many sources: they are historical data and belong to nobody. That is why they are cited in the sources section.
- **The wording, structure, analysis tables, reasoning (🧠), conclusions and glossary are original work** for this study, written from the sources without copying their sentences.
- Check by **sampling**: verbatim sentences from the document were searched online and did not appear on other websites. This is a partial test, not a full plagiarism analysis.
- The images come from **Wikimedia Commons** and are almost all **public domain** (1912 photographs or images by US government agencies such as NOAA and the Coast Guard). Copyrighted images (Magellan's 3D scan, RMST's 2024 photos) **are not reproduced**; they are only described and linked.

## 18. Limitations of this study

1. **No access to the raw data** of the 3D scan (privately owned); the published conclusions are used.
2. **The wreck's condition is changing**: the last extensive documentation dates from 2024. No major known expeditions took place in 2025 or 2026.
3. **Victim figures**: an exact number is impossible.
4. **Predictions of disappearance**: highly uncertain; no scientific model is conclusive.
5. **Causes of the sinking**: the rivet theory is based on the analysis of a handful of rivets (out of about 3 million).
6. **Ethical debate**: the conclusions in section 14.5 are a reasoned opinion, not a fact.
